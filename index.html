    How the fit score works

    1. Your answers pick the factors that matter for your company and how much each one counts: Low counts once (1×), Medium twice (2×), and High three times (3×). A factor's share of the score is its weight divided by the total. For example, one High, two Medium, and one Low factor add up to 8, so the High factor is 3 of 8, or 38% of the score. You can change any of this under "Change weights or add a factor".
    2. For each factor, every US county gets a percentile from 0 to 100: where it stands against every other county. Where less is better, such as few annual wellness visits, the percentile is flipped.
    3. The fit score is the weighted average of those percentiles. A county needs data on every High (3×) factor, and on at least two-thirds of the weight overall, to get a score. When a lower-weight factor has no data, the score uses the others, and the county's profile says which one is missing.
    4. The filters (state, minimum residents, and metro or rural) narrow the ranking. They don't change any county's score, so a 90 means the same thing in every view.

    How each number is calculated

    • Where the 30-mile circle starts. Each county's circle starts at its center of population: the average location of everyone who lives there, from the 2020 Census. I use these instead of geographic centers so the circle starts where people live. San Bernardino County's center of population sits about 80 miles from the middle of its land, among the cities of its southwest corner rather than out in the Mojave Desert. Connecticut's planning regions are newer than the 2020 centers, so they use a Census point near each region's middle.
    • Within 30 miles. A county's 30-mile total adds up every county whose center is within 30 straight-line miles of its own, itself included. Each of those counties counts in full, and one just past 30 miles counts not at all. The total crosses state lines even when you show one state: Bergen County, NJ, adds up 14 counties in New Jersey and New York, Manhattan included. Senior living communities, home care businesses, and nursing homes within 30 miles are counted the same way.
    • The catch is county size. Los Angeles County's center is 32 miles from Orange County's, so its 30-mile total counts only itself, as do 342 of the 3,144 counties. Treat the total as a rough service area: distance as the crow flies, not driving time.
    • Per square mile. Residents in the age group divided by the county's land area, water left out. It covers the county alone and averages over all of its land, so big counties with empty stretches look sparse: San Bernardino County has 97,162 residents 75–84 but only 4.8 per square mile across 20,068 square miles.
    • Share of residents. Residents in the age group divided by the county's total population, both on July 1, 2025.
    • Growth since 2020. The percent change in residents in the age group from July 1, 2020, to July 1, 2025, both from the same Census estimates release, so the two years are comparable.
    • Percentiles. A county's percentile on a factor compares it with every US county that has data on that factor, whatever state or filters you pick. The lowest value gets 0 and the highest 100, and counties with the same value share the percentile in the middle of their group.
    • Ties in the ranking. Counties with the same fit score are ordered by residents in the age group within 30 miles, most first.
    • Minimum residents. Counts the county's own residents in the age group, not its 30-mile total.
    • Senior living communities per 1,000 residents 75+. This factor, under "Add a factor", always divides by residents 75+, whatever age group you pick.

    Definitions and gaps

    • Age groups. The survey uses Urban Institute's own age groups (65–74, 75–84, and 85+), so every factor describes the same people: Census counts and Urban's money and health figures for the ages you pick. Each factor's label names the ages it uses. The Explore tab shows residents 75+ (75–84 plus 85+).
    • Net worth for ages 85+. Urban reports 85+ median net worth for only 357 counties. For the 85+ group, the score uses the 75–84 figure where the 85+ one is missing, marked with an asterisk. Where both exist, the 85+ figure is typically about 6% lower (the middle half of counties run from 18% lower to 3% higher), so filled-in counties get a slight edge. Other measures differ too much by age to mix this way. Median income, for example, runs about a quarter lower at 85+, so those gaps stay empty.
    • Senior living communities and home care businesses. Senior living combines two Census business categories: assisted living facilities and continuing care retirement communities. Home care combines non-medical services for older adults and people with disabilities (which also takes in some adult day programs) with home health services. Both count business locations with paid staff, so a 200-unit community and a 20-unit one each count once. Businesses that Census lists only statewide are left out. Census also leaves out any county where a category has fewer than three locations, so a county shown with 0 may have 1 or 2, and the 30-mile totals can miss them too.
    • Medicare Advantage share. Medicare Advantage enrollees divided by everyone eligible for Medicare in the county, at all ages. The November 2026 CMS file leaves out Alaska and Connecticut while their county codes are updated, and blanks counties with 10 or fewer enrollees.
    • Metro and rural. Question 4 uses USDA's Rural-Urban Continuum Codes. Metro counties are codes 1–3, and rural and small-town counties are the nonmetro codes 4–9.
    • Why Medicaid isn't a payer option. Medicaid is run state by state. Each state sets who qualifies, which home and community services it covers, whether managed care plans run long-term care, and what it pays. None of that shows up in county data, so a county could score well in a state that doesn't pay for your service at all. The older-adult poverty rate, which shows where people might qualify, is still available under "Add a factor".
    • Regional estimates. Urban’s employment and wealth figures (net worth, income, poverty, emergency savings, housing costs, and work) repeat exactly across neighboring counties, and the groups match the Census Bureau’s Public Use Microdata Areas (PUMAs), areas of at least 100,000 people. Garfield, Moffat, Rio Blanco, and Routt counties in Colorado, for example, form one PUMA and share one median net worth. About 4 in 5 counties share their median net worth with at least one other county, as many as 24 in the Texas Panhandle. Large counties, and counties a PUMA line cuts through, have their own. So on these factors, small neighboring counties can’t be told apart. Urban hasn’t yet published its method for these figures.
    • Connecticut. Connecticut's planning regions have Census, CMS nursing home, business, employment, and wealth figures. Urban reports the state's other metrics for its former eight counties, which Census no longer publishes population or area for, so those metrics are blank for Connecticut.
    • Labels. "Share employed" is labeled "NA" in Urban's file. It's the one employment metric in Urban's data dictionary with no other name in the file. I also corrected two misspelled labels: depressive symptoms and pneumococcal vaccine.
