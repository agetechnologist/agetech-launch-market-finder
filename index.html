    How the fit score works

    1. Your answers pick the factors that matter for your company and how much each one counts: Low counts once (1×), Medium twice (2×) and High three times (3×). A factor's share of the score is its weight divided by the total. For example, one High, two Medium and one Low factor add up to 8, so the High factor is 3 of 8, or 38% of the score. You can change the factors under "What the score counts".
    2. For each factor, every US county gets a percentile from 0 to 100: where it stands against every other county. Where less is better, such as few annual wellness visits, the percentile is flipped.
    3. The fit score is the weighted average of those percentiles. A county needs data on every High (3×) factor, and on at least two-thirds of the weight overall, to get a score. When a lower-weight factor has no data, the score uses the others, and the county's profile says which one is missing.
    4. The state, size and metro or rural choices narrow the ranking. They don't change any county's score, so a 90 means the same in every view.

    Definitions and gaps

    • A shortlist, not an answer. The fit score ranks counties against each other on the factors your answers set. It doesn't measure competition, regulation or real demand for a product, which this data doesn't cover.
    • Age groups. Urban Institute doesn't report a 75+ group for most metrics, so wealth, income and health factors use the closest group it does report: ages 75–84 for 75+, 85+ for 85+, and 65+ (or 65–74 where Urban has no 65+ group) for 65+. Each factor's label names the ages used.
    • Within 30 miles. Adds up every county whose Census internal point is within 30 straight-line miles of this county's. It stands in for a service area, and it is rough for very large counties, which are common in the West. Los Angeles County's internal point is far enough north that its 30-mile reach counts only itself.
    • Senior living communities and home care businesses. Senior living is assisted living facilities for the elderly plus continuing care retirement communities. Home care is services for the elderly and persons with disabilities (mostly non-medical home care, with some adult day and other services) plus home health care. Both count locations with paid staff, not beds or clients, and leave out businesses Census lists statewide rather than in a county.
    • Medicare Advantage share. Medicare Advantage enrollees divided by everyone eligible for Medicare in the county, at all ages. CMS left out Alaska and Connecticut this month while their county codes are updated, and blanks counties with 10 or fewer enrollees.
    • Metro and rural. Question 4 uses USDA's Rural-Urban Continuum Codes. Metro counties are codes 1–3; rural and small-town counties are the nonmetro codes 4–9.
    • Medicaid. Eligibility and home-care programs vary by state, and this data doesn't cover them. The poverty rate stands in for how many older adults may qualify.
    • Repeated values. Many employment, wealth and debt figures repeat exactly across neighboring counties; four Colorado mountain counties share one median net worth, for example. That suggests Urban estimated those metrics for groups of counties, so treat differences between small neighboring counties with care.
    • Connecticut. Appears only for Census, CMS nursing home, business and employment and wealth figures. Urban reports Connecticut's other metrics for its former eight counties, which Census no longer publishes population or area for.
    • Labels. "Share employed" is labeled "NA" in Urban's file; it is the one employment metric in Urban's data dictionary with no other name in the file. Two misspelled labels are corrected: depressive symptoms and pneumococcal vaccine.
